# "You Had to be There" — full reference script

**Not your script.** A full-length reference so you can read at your own cadence,
time it, and then rewrite in your own words. Your voice is the authority.

Matches the deck as of 13 Oct 2026. Slide numbers in brackets.

**[1]**

⟨A⟩ Two nights ago Jamie and I were walking back from dinner, and we passed a
man on Lygon Street having a complete, furious argument with a parking meter.
And he —

*(beat)*

No. You had to be there.

That's the phrase. And it's an apology. It means: *I had something. I carried it
all the way over here. And it didn't survive the trip.*

Every fact was in it. I told it right. It died anyway.

**[2]**

Hi. I'm Justin Fritz. And I'm a Nerd Nite Boss.

That's a real title. The organisation gave it to me, and I take it very
seriously.

Mostly it means getting out of the way.

**[3]**

I've had a lot of jobs. I was a Marine — a navigator on C-130s, and then I
taught other people to do it. I'm an electrical engineer, bachelor's and
master's; the master's was in wireless. Then about twenty years designing the
insides of computer chips — some of that is in a Microsoft headset, some of it
is in data centres you've used today without knowing. I started a company. And
I host a monthly show in a publick house in Colorado.

As an American, I tend to conflate the person with the job. Ask an American what
they *are* and they'll tell you what they *do*. It's a whole thing.

So let me be clear about what that list actually is. It isn't a resume. It's a
list of rooms I was lucky to be in.

**[4]**

And the education that actually mattered didn't happen in a classroom. It
happened in the field. In the lab. And frankly — at Nerd Nites. ⟨B⟩ I have
learned more on a Thursday night from a stranger with a slide deck than I did in
some entire semesters.

**[5]**

Everything I just showed you is a sample of one trajectory. Everyone in this
room has one. Just as long, just as strange — and I don't know any of yours.

But tonight they crossed. All of them. In here.

Thank you. And I mean all of it — everyone who stands up, everyone who makes it
happen, and everyone who just comes.

*(pause — let it sit)*

**[6]**

Talks. Mars. An abstract. An organ.

**[7]**

Back to the phrase.

"You had to be there" is what you say when the description didn't carry the
energy. Not the facts. The energy. You can get every single fact across and
still lose the thing entirely.

And tonight it means something else as well — because for most of what I'm about
to tell you, **I wasn't there either.** You had to be at the *talk*.

**[8]**

January last year. Hannah Bauer — she's one of our bosses — gets up and gives a
talk called *Worst Party Ever: Everything You Didn't Know About the Donner
Party.*

For anyone who doesn't know: a group of American settlers, 1846, take a shortcut
over the Sierra Nevada. They get caught by snow. And they eat each other.

Hannah's opening was: "Most people hear *Donner Party* and think *cannibalism*.
Valid. But that only scratches the surface."

And she was right, because the talk isn't about that. ⟨C⟩ It's about the
decisions. It's about the shortcut — which came from a man named Lansford
Hastings, who had written a book recommending a route he had never actually
travelled. Hannah put his face on a slide with the word VILLAIN over it.

⟨D⟩ And for twenty minutes she walked us through it the way you'd walk through a
bad engineering decision. What they knew. When they knew it. What they chose
anyway.

And we laughed. We sat in a room in Fort Collins and laughed about the Donner
Party. Not at them — at the absurdity of it.

Nobody at Wolverine Farm was in the Sierra Nevada in 1846. For about twenty
minutes, they were.

**[9]**

Another one. April 2024. Matt Browne.

*Hector Berlioz Was a Visionary Composer and also a Creepy Stalker.*

*(say nothing)*

**[10]**

Now — let's be honest about what's happening in this room.

You weren't at the Donner Party.

You weren't at the talk about the Donner Party.

You are at a talk **about a talk** about the Donner Party.

That's three steps out. A photocopy of a photocopy of a photocopy. And some of
you just laughed anyway.

So why does it still work?

*(ask it. move on.)*

**[11]**

We have never sent a person to Mars.

We've sent probes. Rovers. Instruments. ⟨E⟩ Curiosity has been up there since
2012, driving around a crater at roughly walking pace for a tortoise, and it has
sent back more data than any human being will ever read. It's all public. You
could start downloading it on the tram home.

And here's the thing about that data. Somebody had a hypothesis before that
rover ever left the ground — a reason to build it, a question they wanted
answered. And somebody else is sitting at a desk right now reading it back, with
a model in their head of what Mars is like.

**[12]**

The data came back.

The people stayed home.

Energy does not transmit. It has to be made again — in a room, by a person —
every single time, or it isn't there at all.

Nobody sent a probe because they needed the data. They sent it because they
**wanted to know.** You can send anyone the dataset. You cannot send them the
wanting.

**[13]**

September last year. A man named John Anderson.

John is a long-time friend of our venue. For years he'd be in on a Thursday
evening having a drink while this nerd thing assembled around him. One night he
walked up to my co-boss Senne and said he'd like to give a talk. About worms.

⟨F⟩ John is not an academic. He drives a van with VISUALIZE GLOBAL WORMING
painted down the side of it.

He sent us an abstract. It was rough. Hard to follow. There's a sentence in it
that just stops partway through. My wife Jamie — also a boss — started cleaning
it up.

And I stopped her.

Because we are not there to filter people. We're there to connect them. So we
ran it exactly as he sent it. Typo and all.

And John got up, dressed exactly the way he dresses for any other Thursday, and
gave a tremendous talk about worms. ⟨G⟩ Darwin's worms. Leaf folding. Sidewalk
surfing. How they reproduce, which is a longer answer than you want.

Here's the thing. Nobody walks into a room on a Thursday night *wanting* to know
about worms. They left wanting to know about worms.

And if Jamie had tidied that abstract, it would have read better and carried
less. Because what was in it was John.

**[14]**

June this year. Joel Bacon. *Let's Build a Pipe Organ!*

Joel is a professor, a composer, and a working musician. And he starts the way
you'd expect — walking us through how an organ works. The wind supply. The keys.
The pipes. ⟨H⟩ He mentions, almost in passing, that these are the most
complicated instruments on earth, and that the design goes back to Roman
antiquity.

And then, while he is still talking, his assistants start handing parts out into
the audience.

Hundreds of them. ⟨I⟩ Pipes. Linkages. Pieces of the wind chest. Going out into
the hands of people who came for a beer and a night out.

And the room starts building.

A real one. Two octaves. Mechanical action. Hand pumped.

**[15]**

And then he sat down at a thing a room full of strangers had just assembled —
and he played it.

*(pause)*

It connected me to the music in a way I had never felt before.

*(pause. nothing.)*

⟨J⟩ And I want to be precise about that, because it wasn't that it sounded
extraordinary. It's that I had my hands on it.

And here's the part I keep coming back to.

A hand-pumped organ cannot make a sound unless somebody is standing there
supplying the air.

No person. No air. No sound.

We needed a volunteer. Somebody put their hand up.

**[16]** *(blank screen)*

*(your twenty seconds)*

You had to be there.

You are.
