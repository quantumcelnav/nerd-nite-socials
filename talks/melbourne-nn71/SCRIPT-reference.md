# "You Had to be There" — reference read-through

**This is not your script.** It is a reference performance written by Claude so
you can read the talk end to end at your own cadence and put a clock on it.
Your voice is the authority. Steal the structure, not the sentences.

Matches the deck as of 13 Oct 2026 — energy thesis, the ladder, getting out of
the way, rooms-not-resume. Slide numbers in brackets.

**Three things in here are invented and must be replaced** — see the end.

---

**[1]**

Last Thursday Jamie and I were on a tram, and a bloke got on with a cockatoo on
his shoulder. And the cockatoo —

*(beat)*

No. You had to be there.

That's the phrase. It's an apology. It means: *I had something, and it didn't
survive the trip over here.* Every fact was in it. I told it right. It died
anyway.

**[2]**

Hi. I'm Justin Fritz. And I'm a Nerd Nite Boss.

That's a real title. The organisation gave it to me, and I take it very
seriously.

Mostly it means getting out of the way.

**[3]**

I've had a lot of jobs. I was a Marine — a navigator on C-130s, and then I
taught navigation. I'm an electrical engineer, bachelor's and master's. I spent
about twenty years designing the insides of computer chips; my master's work was
in wireless. I started a company. And I host a monthly show in a publick house
in Colorado.

As an American, I tend to conflate the person with the job. Ask an American what
they *are* and they'll tell you what they *do*.

So let me be clear about what that list is. It isn't a resume. It's a list of
rooms I was lucky to be in.

**[4]**

And the education that actually mattered didn't happen in a classroom. It
happened in the field. In the lab. And frankly — at Nerd Nites.

**[5]**

Everything I just showed you is a sample of one trajectory. Everyone in this
room has one. Just as long. Just as strange.

Tonight ours crossed.

Thank you.

*(pause — let it sit)*

**[6]**

Talks. Mars. An abstract. An organ.

**[7]**

Back to the phrase.

"You had to be there" is what you say when the description didn't carry the
energy. Not the facts. The energy. You can get every single fact across and
still lose the thing.

And tonight it means something else as well — because for most of what I'm about
to tell you, **I wasn't there either.** You had to be at the *talk*.

**[8]**

January last year. Hannah Bauer gets up and gives a talk called *Worst Party
Ever: Everything You Didn't Know About the Donner Party.*

For anyone who doesn't know — a group of American settlers, 1846, take a
shortcut over the Sierra Nevada, get caught by snow, and eat each other.

Hannah's opening was: "Most people hear *Donner Party* and think *cannibalism*.
Valid. But that only scratches the surface."

And then for twenty minutes she put us inside their heads. Not the horror — the
*decisions*. The bad map. The man who sold them the shortcut.

And we laughed. We sat in a room in Fort Collins and laughed about the Donner
Party.

Nobody at Wolverine Farm was in the Sierra Nevada in 1846. For about twenty
minutes, they were.

**[9]**

Another one. April 2024. Matt Browne.

*Hector Berlioz Was a Visionary Composer and also a Creepy Stalker.*

*(say nothing)*

**[10]**

Now — let's be honest about what's happening in this room.

You weren't at the Donner Party.

You weren't at the talk about the Donner Party.

You are at a talk **about a talk** about the Donner Party.

That's three steps out. A photocopy of a photocopy of a photocopy. And some of
you just laughed anyway.

So why does it still work?

*(ask it. move on.)*

**[11]**

We have never sent a person to Mars.

We've sent probes. Rovers. Instruments. They have returned more data than anyone
will ever read. It's all there, it's public, you could download it tonight.

Somebody had a hypothesis before that thing ever left the ground. And somebody
else is at a desk right now reading it back, with a model in their head of what
Mars is.

**[12]**

The data came back.

The people stayed home.

Energy does not transmit. It has to be made again — in a room, by a person —
every single time, or it isn't there at all.

Nobody sent a probe because they needed the data. They sent it because they
**wanted to know**. You can send anyone the dataset. You cannot send them the
wanting.

**[13]**

September last year. A man named John Anderson.

John's a long-time friend of our venue. For years he'd be in on a Thursday
having a drink, watching this nerd thing assemble around him. One night he
walked up to my co-boss Senne and said he'd like to give a talk. About worms.

He sent us an abstract. It was rough. Hard to follow. My wife Jamie — also a
boss — started cleaning it up.

And I stopped her.

Because we're not there to filter people. We're there to connect them. So we ran
it exactly as he sent it.

And John got up, dressed exactly the way he dresses for any other Thursday, and
gave a tremendous talk about worms.

Here's the thing. Nobody walks into a room on a Thursday night *wanting* to know
about worms. They left wanting to know about worms. And if Jamie had tidied that
abstract, it would have read better and carried less — because what was in it
was John.

**[14]**

June this year. Joel Bacon. *Let's Build a Pipe Organ!*

Joel's a professor and a working musician. He starts walking us through how an
organ works — the wind supply, the keys, the pipes.

And while he's talking, his assistants start handing parts out into the
audience. Hundreds of them. And the room starts building.

A real one. Two octaves. Mechanical action. Hand pumped.

**[15]**

And then he sat down and he played it.

It connected me to the music in a way I had never felt before.

*(pause. nothing.)*

A hand-pumped organ cannot make a sound unless somebody is standing there
supplying the air.

No person. No air. No sound.

We needed a volunteer. Somebody put their hand up.

**[16]** *(blank screen)*

*(your twenty seconds)*

You had to be there.

You are.

## Timing

**1,090 words.** At a measured 115–120 wpm that is about **9:30 of speech**. The
remaining 4–5 minutes are pauses, laughs and slide changes, which is why the
deck budgets 14:15 and why the pause marks matter more than the word count.

Time it twice. The first run comes in long; that is normal. **If you land over
16:00, cut Berlioz** — slide 9, thirty seconds, the only genuinely optional
beat in the talk.

## Three things in here are invented and are not yours

1. **The cockatoo.** Placeholder. You need a real small story that died on
   retelling. This is still the biggest open item in the talk.
2. **The Donner details** — 1846, the Sierra Nevada, "the man who sold them the
   shortcut." Directionally right, unverified by me, and you are the one
   standing there.
3. **Hannah's opening line.** That is from her written abstract, not a
   transcript of what she said. Either verify it or say "her abstract said."
